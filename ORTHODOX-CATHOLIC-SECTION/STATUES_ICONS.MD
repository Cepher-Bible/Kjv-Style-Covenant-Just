**The Myth of the "Bare Walls" Bible**

The Second Commandment says in Hebrew: “You shall not make for yourself (*lo ta’aseh lecha*) a carved image (*pesel*)… You shall not bow down to them or serve them.”

The word *pesel* is used in recurring contexts of carved objects treated as recipients of worship, bowing, or service — actions that belong to the Lord alone. The prohibition focuses on the transfer of *avodah* — service — from God to the object itself.

Just chapters later, the same God commands two golden cherubim to be hammered out for the Ark of the Covenant and woven into the Tabernacle curtains. These carved figures appear only in construction and placement instructions, never as recipients of cultic action, and are never labeled *pesel*.

In Numbers 21, God commands Moses to make a bronze serpent so the people can look at it and live. It functions as a commanded sign. Centuries later, when the Israelites begin burning incense to it and treating it as an object of cultic service, Hezekiah destroys it. The same object shifts from commanded sign to an object of cultic service that is subsequently destroyed.

When Joshua and the elders fall on their faces before the Ark, the text shows them humbling themselves before the Lord whose covenant presence the Ark represents. The decisive factor is whether the act of bowing or service terminates at the object or remains directed to the God who authorized it.

The Hebrew Bible therefore does not forbid material representation as such. It forbids relocating *avodah* — service and trust — from the living God to a carved thing.

That single boundary explains every image in the Torah without contradiction.
