Brief, unified form.

The covenant story begins with image, not prohibition. In Genesis 1 humanity is created as the living image of God. The second commandment in Exodus 20 does not introduce hostility to representation; it guards divine identity from distortion and replacement. The prohibition is against rival gods and fabricated deities, not against all visual form in service of the LORD.

This is confirmed immediately in the Tabernacle. In Exodus 25 God commands cherubim over the mercy seat. In 1 Kings 6–7 the Temple is filled with carved cherubim, palms, lions, bulls. These are not violations of the commandment; they are commanded sacred art within covenant worship. Therefore the commandment cannot mean “no sacred imagery whatsoever.” It means no substitute gods and no visible containment of the invisible LORD.

Numbers 21 establishes the governing pattern. God commands the bronze serpent. It is crafted, lifted up, and becomes the appointed instrument through which healing flows. The bronze itself has no power; God’s word activates it. When the people later burn incense to it (2 Kings 18:4), it is destroyed. The object was not evil; the heart shifted. This establishes the controlling safeguard: signposts are legitimate; substitutes are not.

The priesthood reinforces this mediation pattern. God appoints human beings to stand representationally between Himself and the people. Mediation in Scripture is derivative and appointed, never independent. God uses created means not because He needs them, but because embodied creatures do.

The prophets then clarify that the movement of covenant history is internalization, not abolition. Deuteronomy 30 already anticipates heart circumcision. Jeremiah 31 and Ezekiel 36 promise the Law written on the heart and the Spirit within. The shift is administrative escalation — from external scaffolding to internal empowerment — not a rejection of embodiment.

The decisive turning point is the Incarnation. In John 1 the Word becomes flesh and tabernacles among us. In Colossians 1 Christ is the Image of the invisible God. In Hebrews 1 He is the exact imprint of God’s being. The prior aniconic restraint guarded divine invisibility. Once God truly assumes visible humanity, depicting Christ’s human form does not fabricate a god; it confesses that God truly took flesh.

This does not create a command to make images. It removes the claim that matter cannot serve devotion. Christianity is irreducibly material: water in baptism, bread and wine in the Supper, spoken words, written Scripture, resurrected bodies. Redemption is not escape from matter but its glorification.

Jesus Himself anchors the bronze serpent typology in John 3. The lifted serpent prefigures the lifted Son. The object representing curse points to the One who bears the curse. In that sense, a crucifix functions analogously: it does not replace Christ; it visually proclaims Him crucified. Its legitimacy depends entirely on whether it directs faith to the living Christ rather than to the object itself.

The New Covenant temple theology intensifies presence rather than abolishing embodiment. Believers collectively and individually become temples of the Spirit (1 Corinthians 3; 6). Revelation culminates with no temple because the Lord God and the Lamb are its temple. The trajectory is increasing immediacy of presence, not dematerialization of worship.

The real prohibition in the New Covenant is internal idolatry. Ezekiel 14 and Colossians 3 identify idols of the heart. One can have no sacred images and still worship self, money, or power. The issue is ultimate trust, not mere physical form.

The transformation arc completes the logic. Psalm 115 teaches that those who worship dead idols become like them. Second Corinthians 3 teaches that beholding the Lord transforms us into His image. False images deform; the true Image reforms. Romans 8 says believers are conformed to the image of the Son. The entire covenant story is image guarded, image promised, image incarnate, image restored, image consummated.

Intercession follows the same covenant continuity. In the Old Covenant priests intercede. In the New Covenant Christ is the one Mediator (1 Timothy 2:5), the eternal High Priest (Hebrews 7). Yet believers are called a royal priesthood (1 Peter 2) and commanded to intercede for one another (1 Timothy 2; James 5). Derivative intercession does not compete with Christ’s unique mediation; it participates in it.

The saints in heaven are alive to God (Luke 20). Revelation 5 and 8 depict heavenly participation in the presentation of prayers. The theological claim, therefore, is not that saints replace Christ, but that they share in His priestly communion. Asking a saint to pray is structurally analogous to asking a believer on earth to pray — provided worship and saving trust remain directed to Christ alone.

Everything stands or falls on the controlling distinctions:

Worship belongs to God alone.
Christ alone mediates salvation.
All other intercession is derivative and dependent.
All material aids are subordinate and optional.

If an object becomes necessary, power-bearing, or independently trusted, it becomes Nehushtan. If it directs the heart toward Christ and can be relinquished without collapsing faith, it remains a tool.

So the unified conclusion is this:

Scripture does not mandate sacred images or requests for saintly intercession.
Scripture does provide covenantal precedent, theological logic, and Christological grounding that make them permissible when rightly ordered.

Your framework is internally consistent because it never shifts worship away from Christ, never multiplies mediators of salvation, and never treats matter as divine. It argues not from novelty, but from covenant continuity fulfilled in the Incarnation.

The arc holds as long as this remains fixed:

Christ is the object of worship.
Christ is the source of salvation.
Everything else is servant, not substitute.
