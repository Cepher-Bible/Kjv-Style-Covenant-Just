\v 1 The Jewish brothers in Jerusalem and those in the land of Judea — to their Jewish brothers in Egypt — greetings and true peace.
\v 2 May God do good to you and remember his covenant with Abraham and Isaac and Jacob — his faithful servants.
\v 3 May he give you all a heart (*kardia*) to worship him and to do his will with a strong heart (*kardia*) and a willing soul (*psychē*).
\v 4 May he open your heart (*kardia*) to his Law and his commandments and send you peace.
\v 5 May he hear your prayers and be reconciled to you and not forsake you in time of evil.
\v 6 We are now praying for you here.
\p
\v 7 In the reign of Demetrius in the one hundred and sixty-ninth year we Jews wrote to you in the critical distress that came upon us in those years after Jason and his company revolted from the holy land and the kingdom —
\v 8 and burned the gate and shed innocent blood. We prayed to the Lord and were heard — and we offered sacrifice and fine flour — we lit the lamps and set out the loaves.
\p
\v 24 And the prayer was in this form: O Lord Lord God creator of all things who are awe-inspiring and strong and just and merciful — the only king and the only bestower of grace —
\v 25 the only just and almighty and eternal one who rescues Israel from every evil who chose our ancestors and consecrated them —
\v 26 accept this sacrifice on behalf of all your people Israel and preserve your portion and make it holy.
\v 27 Gather together our scattered people — set free those who are slaves among the Gentiles look on those who are rejected and despised and let the Gentiles know that you are our God.
\v 28 Afflict those who oppress us and are insolent with pride.
\v 29 Plant your people in your holy place as Moses promised.

\p
\v 13 The same things are reported in the records and in the memoirs of Nehemiah — and also that he founded a library and collected the books about the kings and prophets and the writings of David and letters of kings about votive offerings.
\v 14 In the same way Judas also collected all the books that had been lost on account of the war that had come upon us — and they are in our possession.
\v 15 So if you have need of them send people to get them for you.
\p
\v 19 The story of Judas Maccabeus and his brothers and the purification of the great temple and the dedication of the altar —
\v 20 and the wars against Antiochus Epiphanes and his son Eupator —
\v 21 and the appearances that came from heaven to those who fought bravely for Judaism so that though few in number they seized the whole land and pursued the barbarian hordes —
\v 22 and recovered the temple famous throughout the whole world and freed the city and re-established the laws that were about to be abolished — while the Lord with great kindness became gracious to them —
\v 23 all this which has been set forth by Jason of Cyrene in five volumes we shall attempt to condense into a single book.

\c 3
\p
\v 1 While the holy city was inhabited in unbroken peace and the laws were strictly observed because of the piety of the high priest Onias and his hatred of wickedness —
\v 2 it came about that the kings themselves honored the place and glorified the temple with the finest presents —
\v 3 even to the point that Seleucus the king of Asia defrayed from his own revenues all the expenses connected with the service of the sacrifices.
\p
\v 14 When the day arrived and Heliodorus appeared with his bodyguard at the treasury there was tense excitement throughout the whole city.
\v 15 The priests prostrated themselves before the altar in their priestly vestments and called toward heaven upon the one who had given the law about deposits saying that he should keep them safe for those who had deposited them.
\v 16 To see the appearance of the high priest was to be wounded at heart — for his face and the change in his color disclosed the anguish of his soul (*psychē*).
\v 17 For terror and bodily trembling had come over the man which plainly showed to those who looked at him the pain lodged in his heart (*kardia*).
\p
\v 24 But when he arrived at the treasury with his bodyguard then and there the Sovereign of spirits and of all authority caused so great a manifestation that all who had been so bold as to accompany him were astounded by the power of God and became faint with terror.
\v 25 For there appeared to them a magnificently caparisoned horse with a rider of frightening mien and it rushed furiously at Heliodorus and struck at him with its front hoofs. Its rider was seen to have armor and weapons of gold.
\v 26 Two young men also appeared to him remarkable in their strength exceedingly beautiful and splendidly dressed who stood on either side of him and flogged him continuously inflicting many blows on him.
\v 27 When he suddenly fell to the ground and deep darkness came over him his men picked him up and put him on a stretcher —
\v 28 and carried him away — this man who had just entered the aforesaid treasury with a great retinue and his whole bodyguard and was now unable to help himself. They recognized clearly the sovereign power of God.
\p
\v 33 While he was being cared for by the young men the former high priest Onias offered prayer for the man fearing that the king might get the notion that this injury was actually perpetrated by the Jews.
\v 34 So the two young men said to Heliodorus: Be very grateful to the high priest Onias since for his sake the Lord has granted you your life.
\v 35 And you having been scourged from heaven report to all people the majestic power of God. Having said this they vanished.
\p
\v 36 Then Heliodorus offered sacrifice to the Lord and made very great vows to the Savior of his life and bade farewell to Onias and returned with his forces to the king.
\v 37 And he testified to all concerning the deeds of the supreme God which he had seen with his own eyes.
\v 38 When the king asked Heliodorus what sort of person would be suitable to send to Jerusalem once more the man replied:
\v 39 If you have any enemy or plotter against your government send him there for you will get him back thoroughly flogged if he survives at all — for there is certainly some power of God about the place.
\v 40 For he who has his dwelling in heaven watches over that place himself and brings it aid and he strikes and destroys those who come to do it injury.

\c 4
\p
\v 13 There was such an extreme of Hellenization and increase in the adoption of foreign ways because of the surpassing wickedness of Jason who was ungodly and no true high priest —
\v 14 that the priests were no longer intent upon their service at the altar. Despising the sanctuary and neglecting the sacrifices they hurried to take part in the unlawful proceedings in the wrestling arena after the signal for the discus-throwing —
\v 15 disdaining the honors prized by their ancestors and putting the highest value upon Greek forms of prestige.

\c 5
\p
\v 15 Not content with this Antiochus dared to enter the most holy temple in all the world guided by Menelaus who had become a traitor both to the laws and to his country.
\v 16 He took the holy vessels with his polluted hands and swept away with profane hands the votive offerings that other kings had made to enhance the glory and honor of the place.
\p
\v 17 Antiochus was elated in spirit and did not perceive that the Lord was angered for a little while because of the sins of those who lived in the city and that this was the reason he was disregarding the holy place.
\v 18 But if it had not happened that they were involved in many sins this man would have been scourged and turned back from his rash act as soon as he came forward just as Heliodorus had been — whom King Seleucus sent to inspect the treasury.
\v 19 But the Lord did not choose the nation for the sake of the holy place — but the place for the sake of the nation.
\v 20 Therefore the place itself shared in the misfortunes that befell the nation — and afterward participated in its benefits — and what was forsaken in the wrath of the Almighty was restored again in all its glory when the great Lord became reconciled.
\p
\v 27 But Judas Maccabeus with about nine others got away to the wilderness — and kept himself and his companions alive in the mountains as wild animals do. They continued to live on what grew wild so that they might not share in the defilement.

\c 6
\p
\v 1 Not long after this the king sent an Athenian senator to compel the Jews to forsake the laws of their ancestors and no longer to live by the laws of God —
\v 2 also to pollute the temple in Jerusalem and to call it the temple of Olympian Zeus and to call the one in Gerizim the temple of Zeus-the-Friend-of-Strangers as did the people who lived in that place.
\v 3 Harsh and utterly grievous was the onslaught of evil.
\v 4 For the temple was filled with debauchery and reveling by the Gentiles who dallied with prostitutes and had intercourse with women within the sacred precincts and besides brought in things for sacrifice that were unfit.
\v 5 The altar was covered with abominable offerings that were forbidden by the laws.
\p
\v 12 Now I urge those who read this book not to be depressed by such calamities but to recognize that these punishments were designed not to destroy but to discipline our people.
\v 13 In fact it is a sign of great kindness not to let the impious alone for long but to punish them immediately.
\v 14 For in the case of the other nations the Lord waits patiently to punish them until they have reached the full measure of their sins — but he does not deal in this way with us —
\v 15 in order that he may not take vengeance on us afterward when our sins have reached their height.
\v 16 Therefore he never withdraws his mercy (*eleos*) from us. Although he disciplines us with calamities he does not forsake his own people.
\p
\v 18 Eleazar one of the leading scribes — an aged man and of noble presence — was being forced to open his mouth to eat swine's flesh.
\v 19 But he welcoming death with honor rather than life with pollution went up to the rack of his own accord —
\v 20 spitting out the flesh as all ought to go who have the courage to refuse things that it is not right to taste even for the natural love of life.
\v 21 Those who were in charge of that unlawful sacrifice took the man aside because of their long acquaintance with him and privately urged him to bring meat of his own providing — such as he could use — and to pretend that he was eating the flesh of the sacrificial meal that had been commanded by the king —
\v 22 so that by doing this he would be saved from death and be treated kindly on account of his old friendship with them.
\v 23 But making a high resolve worthy of his years and the dignity of his old age and the gray hairs that he had reached with distinction and his excellent life even from childhood and more than this the holy God-given law he declared himself quickly saying that he would go to Hades.
\v 24 Such pretense is not worthy of our time of life he said — lest many of the young should suppose that Eleazar in his ninetieth year has gone over to an alien religion —
\v 25 and through my pretense for the sake of living a brief moment longer they should be led astray because of me while I defile and disgrace my old age.
\v 26 Even if for the present I would avoid the punishment of mortals yet whether I live or die I shall not escape the hands of the Almighty.
\v 27 Therefore by bravely giving up my life now I will show myself worthy of my old age —
\v 28 and leave to the young a noble example of how to die a good death willingly and nobly for the revered and holy laws.
\p When he had said this he went immediately to the rack.
\v 29 And those who a little before had acted toward him with goodwill now changed to ill will because the words he had uttered were in their opinion sheer madness.
\v 30 When he was about to die under the blows he groaned aloud and said: It is clear to the Lord in his holy knowledge that though I might have been saved from death I am enduring terrible sufferings in my body under this beating — but in my soul (*psychē*) I am glad to suffer these things because I fear him.
\v 31 So in this way he died — leaving in his death an example of nobility and a memorial of courage not only to the young but to the great body of his nation.

\v 1 It happened also that seven brothers and their mother were arrested and were being compelled by the king under torture with whips and cords to partake of unlawful swine's flesh.
\v 2 One of them acting as their spokesman said: What do you intend to ask and learn from us? We are ready to die rather than transgress the laws of our ancestors.
\p
\v 3 The king fell into a rage and gave orders to have pans and caldrons heated.
\v 4 These were heated immediately and he commanded that the tongue of their spokesman be cut out and that they scalp him and cut off his hands and feet while the rest of the brothers and the mother looked on.
\v 5 When he was utterly helpless the king ordered them to take him to the fire still breathing and to fry him in a pan. As the smoke spread widely from the pan a number of his companions together with their mother urged one another with noble resolve to die manfully saying:
\v 6 The Lord God is watching over us and in truth has compassion on us as Moses declared in his song that bears witness against the people to their faces when he said: And he will have compassion on his servants.
\p
\v 7 After the first brother had died in this way they brought forward the second for their sport. They tore off the skin of his head with the hair and asked him: Will you eat rather than have your body punished limb by limb?
\v 8 He replied in the language of his ancestors: No. Therefore he in turn underwent the rest of the torture as the first brother had done.
\v 9 And when he was at his last breath he said: You accursed wretch — you dismiss us from this present life but the King of the universe will raise us up to an everlasting renewal of life (*anastasōs aiōniou* ... *anazēsomenous*) since we have died for his laws.
\p
\v 10 After him the third was made a victim of their sport. When it was demanded he quickly put out his tongue and courageously stretched forth his hands —
\v 11 and said nobly: I got these from Heaven and because of his laws I disdain them and from him I hope to get them back again (*tauta de para tou ouranou elabe... kai para autou tauta palin elpizō komizasthai*).
\p
\v 13 When he too had died they maltreated and tortured the fourth in the same way.
\v 14 When he was near death he said: One cannot but choose to die at the hands of mortals and to cherish the hope that God gives of being raised again by him. But for you there will be no resurrection to life (*soi de anastasis eis zōēn ouk estai*).
\p
\v 20 The mother was especially admirable and worthy of honorable memory. Although she saw her seven sons perish within a single day she bore it with good courage because of her hope in the Lord.
\v 21 She encouraged each of them in the language of their ancestors. Filled with a noble spirit she reinforced her woman's reasoning with a man's courage and said to them:
\v 22 I do not know how you came into being in my womb. It was not I who gave you life and breath and did not I who set in order the elements within each of you.
\v 23 Therefore the Creator of the world who shaped the beginning of humankind and devised the origin of all things will in his mercy give life and breath back to you again since you now forget yourselves for the sake of his laws.
\p
\v 24 Antiochus felt that he was being treated with contempt and he was suspicious of the reproachful voice. As the youngest brother was still alive Antiochus not only appealed to him in words but promised with oaths that he would make him rich and enviable if he would turn from the ways of his ancestors and that he would take him for his Friend and entrust him with public affairs.
\v 26 Since the young man would not listen to him at all the king called the mother and urged her to advise the youth to save himself.
\v 27 After much urging on his part she undertook to persuade her son.
\v 28 But leaning close to him she spoke in their ancestral language as follows: My son have pity on me. I carried you nine months in my womb and nursed you for three years and have reared you and brought you up to this point in your life and have taken care of you.
\v 28 I beg you my child to look at the heaven and the earth and see everything that is in them and recognize that God did not make them out of things that existed (*ouk ex ontōn epoiēsen auta ho theos*). And in the same way the human race came into being.
\v 29 Do not fear this butcher but prove worthy of your brothers. Accept death so that in God's mercy I may get you back again together with your brothers.
\p
\v 30 While she was still speaking the young man said: What are you waiting for? I will not obey the king's command but I will obey the command of the Law that was given to our ancestors through Moses.
\v 31 But you who have contrived all sorts of evil against the Hebrews will certainly not escape the hands of God.
\v 32 For we are suffering because of our own sins.
\v 33 And if our living Lord is angry for a little while to rebuke and discipline us he will again be reconciled with his own servants.
\v 34 But you unholy wretch you most defiled of all mortals do not be carried away with your arrogant and uncertain hopes when you raise your hand against his servants.
\v 35 For you have not yet escaped the judgment of the almighty all-seeing God.
\v 36 For our brothers after enduring a brief suffering have drunk of ever-flowing life under God's covenant — but you through the judgment of God will receive just punishment for your arrogance.
\v 37 I as my brothers do give up body and life for the laws of our ancestors — appealing to God to show mercy soon to our nation and by trials and plagues to make you confess that he alone is God —
\v 38 and through me and my brothers to bring to an end the wrath of the Almighty that has justly fallen on our whole nation.
\p

\v 40 So he died in his integrity putting his whole trust in the Lord.
\p

\v 42 Let this be enough then about the eating of sacrifices and the extreme tortures.

\p

\v 14 But Judas and his men met the enemy in battle — and the Lord looked with favor on them.

\v 5 But the all-seeing Lord the God of Israel struck him with an incurable and invisible blow. As soon as he ceased speaking a pain in his bowels that was irremediable seized him and severe internal tortures —
\v 6 and that very justly for he had tortured the bowels of others with many and strange inflictions.
\v 7 Yet he did not in any way stop his insolence but was even more filled with arrogance breathing fire in his rage against the Jews and giving orders to hasten the journey. \v 8 Thus he who only a little while before had thought in his superhuman arrogance that he could command the waves of the sea and had imagined that he could weigh the high mountains in a balance was brought down to earth and carried in a litter showing to all that the power was God's.

\v 3 They purified the sanctuary and made another altar of sacrifice. Then striking fire out of flint they offered sacrifices after a lapse of two years and they offered incense and lighted lamps and set out the bread of the Presence.
\v 4 When they had done this they fell prostrate and implored the Lord that they might never again fall into such misfortunes but that if they ever did sin they might be disciplined by him with forbearance and not be handed over to blasphemous and barbarous nations.

## CRITICAL TEXTUAL NOTES — 2 MACCABEES

**Literary genre and style:** 2 Maccabees is not a continuation of 1 Maccabees but a parallel and independent account of the same period (175–161 BCE), condensed from a five-volume work by Jason of Cyrene (2:23). It is written in literary Greek (*pathetic historiography*) — rhetorically polished, theologically explicit, emotionally engaged. Its style is sharply different from the Hebraizing Greek of 1 Maccabees.

**Theological register:** Where 1 Maccabees is theologically implicit (no miracles named, no divine speeches, history-as-reported), 2 Maccabees is theologically explicit: divine interventions are named and described (the Heliodorus episode / the angelic horsemen / the dream of chapter 15) and the theological interpretation of suffering is given directly.

**The resurrection theology:** 2 Maccabees is the clearest pre-NT statement of bodily resurrection in the LXX. The martyred brothers declare belief in bodily resurrection (7:9, 11, 14, 23, 29) — *the King of the universe will raise us up* / *I hope to get my limbs back again* / *for you there will be no resurrection to life*. The mothers' speech contains the first explicit statement of *creatio ex nihilo* in the canon (7:28 — *God did not make them out of things that existed*). Both doctrines — resurrection and creation from nothing — are stated for the first time in their clearest OT-canonical form in this chapter.

**The prayer for the dead:** 2 Maccabees 12:43–45 is the canon's only explicit statement of the theological rationale for praying for the dead — grounded in the resurrection hope. The passage is the source for the Catholic/Orthodox practice of praying for the departed.

## CAE NODES — 2 MACCABEES

### 2 Maccabees 5:19–20 — the place for the sake of the nation / the sanctuary as covenant-sign — CAE PRIMARY NODE

**5:19–20** — "But the Lord did not choose the nation for the sake of the holy place — but the place for the sake of the nation. Therefore the place itself shared in the misfortunes that befell the nation and afterward participated in its benefits — and what was forsaken in the wrath of the Almighty was restored again in all its glory when the great Lord became reconciled."

***ouk gar dia ton topon to ethnos all' dia to ethnos ton topon ho Kyrios exelexato***

= for not for the sake of the place did the Lord choose the nation but for the sake of the nation the place.

This is the most theologically precise statement about the relationship between the Temple and the covenant-people in the entire LXX. The common assumption — that the Temple is so sacred that God protects it at all costs — is directly reversed: the Temple shares the fate of the nation. The Temple's defilement is not a defeat for God but a consequence of the nation's sin. The Temple is instrumental; the covenant-people are primary.

The structural theology:
- **The priority sequence**: nation → place (not place → nation)
- **The solidarity principle**: the place participates in the nation's misfortunes AND its benefits
- **The restoration promise**: when the Lord becomes reconciled (*hilastheis* = when propitiation is made) the glory is restored

This verse is the theological key that unlocks the entire sanctuary-desecration / sanctuary-restoration arc from Solomon's Temple through the Babylonian destruction through the Antiochene desecration through the Maccabean cleansing. In every case: the place follows the people.

**Jeremiah 7:4–7** — "Do not trust in these deceptive words: This is the temple of the Lord the temple of the Lord the temple of the Lord... If you truly amend your ways and your deeds... then I will let you dwell in this place." Jeremiah's Temple Sermon is the prophetic antecedent: the Temple is not a magic protection independent of the people's covenant-status. The place does not save those who have abandoned the covenant.

**John 2:19–21** — "Destroy this temple and in three days I will raise it up... But he was speaking about the temple of his body." Jesus's body-Temple declaration is the ultimate fulfillment of the 2 Maccabees 5:19 theology: the place (Temple) was chosen for the sake of the nation / but the ultimate *nation* is the one who embodies it / the Temple that matters is the body of the Son. When the Lord becomes reconciled (*hilastheis*) through the body of the Son the glory is restored — not in a stone building but in the resurrection.

**Acts 7:48–50** — "The Most High does not dwell in houses made by hands... Heaven is my throne and earth is my footstool — what kind of house will you build for me?" Stephen's speech deploys the same theology: the place was never the primary category. The Stephen-speech is the NT homiletical equivalent of 2 Maccabees 5:19.

### 2 Maccabees 6:12–16 — discipline not destruction / the Lord never withdraws *eleos* — CAE PRIMARY NODE

**6:12–16** — "Now I urge those who read this book not to be depressed by such calamities but to recognize that these punishments were designed not to destroy but to discipline our people... in order that he may not take vengeance on us afterward when our sins have reached their height. Therefore he never withdraws his mercy (*eleos*) from us. Although he disciplines us with calamities he does not forsake his own people."

***ho de Kyrios to eleos ou proietai aph' hēmōn***

= but the Lord does not abandon his mercy (*eleos*) from us.

The 2 Maccabees theology of suffering is the most developed in the deuterocanon:

**1. Discipline not destruction** (6:12–13): The suffering is *paideia* = discipline / child-training — not punishment designed to destroy. The parent-child analogy is implicit: God disciplines those he loves.

**2. The timing argument** (6:14–15): For other nations the Lord waits until sin is full before punishing — for Israel he disciplines immediately. The immediate discipline is a sign of the covenant relationship: only those who belong to someone are disciplined by them. The nations are left to accumulate sin; Israel is corrected in the process.

**3. The *eleos* guarantee** (6:16): Despite the discipline — *he does not abandon his eleos from us*. The steadfast love is not suspended during the discipline. The suffering and the *eleos* coexist: God disciplines precisely because he has not abandoned his covenant-commitment.

**Proverbs 3:11–12** — "Do not despise the Lord's discipline or be weary of his reproof — for the Lord reproves him whom he loves as a father the son in whom he delights." The wisdom-tradition ground for the 2 Maccabees theology.

**Hebrews 12:5–11** — "Have you forgotten the exhortation that addresses you as sons: My son do not regard lightly the discipline of the Lord nor be weary when reproved by him. For the Lord disciplines the one he loves and chastises every son whom he receives... He disciplines us for our good that we may share his holiness. For the moment all discipline seems painful rather than pleasant but later it yields the peaceful fruit of righteousness to those who have been trained by it." The Hebrews passage is the NT theological expansion of 2 Maccabees 6:12–16 citing the same Proverbs 3 tradition. The argument structure is identical: suffering = *paideia* / discipline = sign of sonship / *eleos* does not fail. The chain: Proverbs 3:11–12 → 2 Maccabees 6:12–16 → Hebrews 12:5–11.

**Romans 8:28** — "And we know that for those who love God all things work together for good." Paul's *panta synergei* is the NT equivalent of the 2 Maccabees discipline-theology: the calamities work together for the good of those who are the Lord's, because the discipline is not abandonment but covenant-formation.

### 2 Maccabees 6:18–31 — Eleazar's martyrdom / death as noble example — CAE PRIMARY NODE

**6:23** — "But making a high resolve worthy of his years and the dignity of his old age and the gray hairs that he had reached with distinction and his excellent life even from childhood and more than this the holy God-given law he declared himself quickly saying that he would go to Hades."

**6:27–28** — "Therefore by bravely giving up my life now I will show myself worthy of my old age — and leave to the young a noble example of how to die a good death willingly and nobly for the revered and holy laws."

**6:30** — "It is clear to the Lord in his holy knowledge that though I might have been saved from death I am enduring terrible sufferings in my body under this beating — but in my soul (*psychē*) I am glad to suffer these things because I fear him."

The Eleazar narrative is the first named martyr-theology in the LXX canon. Three elements define it:

**1. The refusal of accommodation** (6:21–25): The suggestion to *pretend* to eat the swine-flesh while actually eating permitted food is rejected not on pragmatic grounds but on theological-pedagogical ones: the pretense would mislead the young. The martyr's death is a teaching act. Eleazar refuses not merely because of personal integrity but because of his *responsibility to the community*.

**2. The God-who-sees** (6:30): *It is clear to the Lord in his holy knowledge* — Eleazar's sufferings are witnessed by the Lord even when no human witnesses can perceive the inner disposition. The appeal to divine knowledge (*epistatai*) is the martyr's anchor: the outcome before humans may be defeat / the outcome before God is vindication.

**3. The *psychē* / *soma* distinction** (6:30): *in my body I suffer / in my soul I am glad*. The body undergoes the torture; the *psychē* = soul/inner person maintains the covenant-joy. This is not a Greek dualism (body-bad / soul-good) but a covenant-anthropology: the *psychē* oriented toward God is not defeated by what is done to the *soma*.

**Isaiah 53:10–11** — "Yet it was the will of the Lord to crush him — he has put him to grief... Out of the anguish of his soul (*psychē*) he shall see and be satisfied." The Servant's *psychē*-anguish and the Servant's seeing-satisfaction is the prophetic archetype for Eleazar's *psychē*-gladness in suffering. Both martyrs suffer in the body while the *psychē* remains oriented toward the divine purpose.

**Hebrews 11:35–36** — "Women received back their dead by resurrection. Some were tortured refusing to accept release so that they might rise again to a better life. Others suffered mocking and flogging." The Hebrews cloud-of-witnesses explicitly references the Maccabean martyrs — *tortured / refusing release* is the Eleazar / seven-brothers pattern. Eleazar is the named type of the unnamed *tines* of Hebrews 11:35.

**Philippians 1:20–21** — "It is my eager expectation and hope that I will not be at all ashamed but that with full courage now as always Christ will be honored in my body whether by life or by death. For to me to live is Christ and to die is gain." Paul's *en tō sōmati mou* = in my body / whether by life or by death is the NT equivalent of Eleazar's *in my body I suffer*: the body is the arena of witness / whether it lives or dies the *psychē* oriented toward God gains.

### 2 Maccabees 7:9–14 / 7:22–29 — the resurrection declarations / *creatio ex nihilo* — CAE CRITICAL NODE

**7:9** — "The King of the universe will raise us up to an everlasting renewal of life (*anastasōs aiōniou*) since we have died for his laws."

**7:11** — "I got these from Heaven and because of his laws I disdain them and from him I hope to get them back again (*para autou tauta palin elpizō komizasthai*)."

**7:14** — "But for you there will be no resurrection to life (*soi de anastasis eis zōēn ouk estai*)."

**7:23** — "Therefore the Creator of the world who shaped the beginning of humankind and devised the origin of all things will in his mercy give life and breath back to you again since you now forget yourselves for the sake of his laws."

**7:28** — "I beg you my child to look at the heaven and the earth and see everything that is in them and recognize that God did not make them out of things that existed (*ouk ex ontōn epoiēsen auta ho theos*)."

The seven brothers' martyrdom in chapter 7 is the theological climax of the entire LXX historical and deuterocanonical sequence — and the most doctrinally dense passage in the pre-NT canon. Two doctrines are stated here for the first time with this clarity in canonical Scripture:

**DOCTRINE 1 — BODILY RESURRECTION (7:9, 11, 14, 23, 29)**

The four resurrection declarations are differentiated by emphasis:

- **Second brother** (7:9): *anastasōs aiōniou* = everlasting renewal of life — the resurrection is *aiōnios* = everlasting, not a return to mortal life.
- **Third brother** (7:11): *elpizō komizasthai* = I hope to get them back — the physical limbs given up in death will be restored. The resurrection is bodily: the specific limbs cut off will be returned.
- **Fourth brother** (7:14): *anastasis eis zōēn ouk estai* = no resurrection to life for you — the resurrection is not universal in its benefit. There is a resurrection and there is also an absence-of-resurrection-to-life. The asymmetry is deliberate: the martyrs will rise to life; the tormentor will not.
- **Mother** (7:23, 29): *the Creator... will give life and breath back* / *in God's mercy I may get you back together with your brothers* — resurrection grounded in the Creator's power and mercy.

**DOCTRINE 2 — *CREATIO EX NIHILO*** (7:28)

***ouk ex ontōn epoiēsen auta ho theos*** = God made them not out of things that existed.

The mother's appeal to *creatio ex nihilo* as the ground for resurrection is theologically precise: if God created the world from nothing then God can restore life from death. The resurrection argument is the creation argument: the same power that brought all things into existence from non-existence can bring the dead from non-existence back to life. *Creatio ex nihilo* is not merely a cosmological statement but a resurrection-ground.

This is the first clear canonical statement of creation from nothing (earlier texts such as Genesis 1 are ambiguous about pre-existing matter). The NT picks up both the *creatio ex nihilo* and the resurrection-from-death as a single theological argument.

**Daniel 12:2** — "And many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake — some to everlasting life and some to shame and everlasting contempt." Daniel's resurrection prophecy is the OT prophetic ground; 2 Maccabees 7 is the first narrative enactment with named individuals declaring personal resurrection hope.

**John 5:28–29** — "Do not marvel at this — for an hour is coming when all who are in the tombs will hear his voice and come out — those who have done good to the resurrection of life (*anastasin zōēs*) and those who have done evil to the resurrection of judgment." Jesus's *anastasis zōēs* = resurrection of life echoes the second brother's *anastasōs aiōniou* = everlasting life and the fourth brother's *anastasis eis zōēn* = resurrection to life. The NT formula is the fulfillment of the Maccabean declaration.

**John 11:24–25** — "Martha said to him: I know that he will rise again in the resurrection on the last day. Jesus said to her: I am the resurrection and the life." Martha's resurrection-hope directly echoes the 2 Maccabees 7 theology — the resurrection at the last day. Jesus's *egō eimi hē anastasis* = I am the resurrection is the ultimate fulfillment: not merely a doctrine about the future but a Person who is present. The *anastasōs aiōniou* that the Maccabean brothers declared as a future hope is declared by Jesus as his own identity.

**Romans 4:17** — "As it is written: I have made you the father of many nations — in the presence of the God in whom he believed who gives life to the dead and calls into existence the things that do not exist (*ta mē onta hōs onta*)." Paul's *ta mē onta hōs onta* = calling into existence the things that do not exist is the *creatio ex nihilo* principle of 2 Maccabees 7:28 applied to the resurrection-faith of Abraham: the God who created from nothing raises the dead. The chain: 2 Maccabees 7:28 (*ouk ex ontōn*) → Romans 4:17 (*ta mē onta hōs onta*).

**1 Corinthians 15:52–54** — "The dead will be raised imperishable and we shall be changed... Death is swallowed up in victory." Paul's resurrection-chapter is the NT sustained theological development of the 2 Maccabees 7 foundation: bodily resurrection / the *soma* transformed / the creation power that overcomes death.

**Hebrews 11:35** — "Women received back their dead by resurrection. Some were tortured refusing to accept release so that they might rise again to a better life (*kreittonos anastaseōs*) ." The *kreittonos anastaseōs* = better resurrection of Hebrews 11:35 is the precise echo of the second brother's *anastasōs aiōniou* = everlasting life-renewal: the Maccabean martyrs refused release because they expected the better resurrection. The Hebrews author is identifying the Maccabean martyrs as the OT witnesses whose hope grounds the NT faith.

### 2 Maccabees 12:43–45 — prayer for the dead / the resurrection ground — CAE PRIMARY NODE

**12:43–45** — "He also took up a collection — man by man — to the amount of two thousand drachmas of silver and sent it to Jerusalem to provide for a sin offering. In doing this he acted very well and honorably taking account of the resurrection. For if he were not expecting that those who had fallen would rise again it would have been superfluous and foolish to pray for the dead. But if he was looking to the splendid reward that is laid up for those who fall asleep in godliness it was a holy and pious thought. Therefore he made atonement for the dead so that they might be delivered from their sin."

***ei mē gar tous peptekotas anastēnai prosdokan, perisson an kai lēron hyparchein to hyper nekrōn proseuchesthai***

= for if he were not expecting the fallen to rise again it would be superfluous and foolish to pray for the dead.

The logic of 12:43–45 is one of the most precisely reasoned arguments in the deuterocanon: the theological practice of praying/making atonement for the dead is grounded in the resurrection-hope. The argument structure is:

**Premise 1**: The men who fell were found with idolatrous tokens — they had sinned.
**Premise 2**: Judas made atonement for them (sin-offering / prayer).
**Implicit premise**: Atonement for the dead is only coherent if the dead can benefit from it.
**Conclusion**: Judas's action demonstrates resurrection-faith — for if there is no resurrection, intercession for the dead is meaningless.

The argument is not *therefore pray for the dead* but *if you believe in the resurrection then praying for the dead is coherent*. The resurrection is the premise of the practice, not a conclusion from it.

**1 Corinthians 15:29** — "Otherwise what do people mean by being baptized on behalf of the dead? If the dead are not raised at all why are people baptized on their behalf?" Paul's argument about baptism for the dead (whatever practice he is referencing) runs exactly the same logical structure as 2 Maccabees 12:44: if there is no resurrection then practices oriented toward the dead are incoherent. Paul's argument is *ad hominem* — he uses the Corinthian practice as evidence that they implicitly believe in resurrection. The logical form is identical to Judas's: *if you do X for the dead you must believe in their resurrection*.

### 2 Maccabees 14:37–46 — Razis the Father of the Jews / Lord of life and breath — CAE NODE

**14:46** — "While still alive calling upon the Lord of life and breath (*kyrieuonta tēs zōēs kai tou pneumatos*) to give them back to him again. In this way he died."

***epikaloumenos ton kyrieuonta tēs zōēs kai tou pneumatos tauta palin autō apodounai***

= calling upon the one who is Lord of life and of the breath-of-life to give these back to him again.

The Razis death-scene is one of the most violent in the deuterocanon — a self-inflicted death to avoid capture. The theological weight is carried by the final petition: Razis calls on the *kyrieuonta tēs zōēs kai tou pneumatos* = the Lord who has lordship over life and the breath-of-life to restore what he is surrendering. The appeal is to the Creator-Redeemer who gave the *pneuma* = breath-of-life and can therefore restore it.

The *tou pneumatos* = of the breath-of-life directly echoes the mother's speech: *the Creator... will give life and breath back to you again* (7:23). Both the mother and Razis appeal to divine sovereignty over the *pneuma* as the ground of resurrection-hope: the *pneuma* given in creation can be given again in resurrection.

**Acts 17:25** — "nor is he served by human hands as though he needed anything since he himself gives to all mankind life and breath and everything." Paul's declaration that God gives *zōēn kai pnoēn* = life and breath to all is the NT equivalent of the *kyrieuonta tēs zōēs kai tou pneumatos* = Lord of life and breath of 2 Maccabees 14:46. The creator who gives breath can restore it.

### 2 Maccabees 15:11–16 — the dream of Onias and Jeremiah — CAE PRIMARY NODE

**15:12–15** — "What he saw was this: Onias who had been high priest — a noble and good man — was praying with outstretched hands for the whole body of the Jews. Then in the same fashion another man appeared — and Onias spoke saying: This is a man who loves the family of Israel and prays much for the people and the holy city — Jeremiah the prophet of God. Jeremiah stretched out his right hand and gave to Judas a golden sword saying: Take this holy sword — a gift from God — with which you will strike down your adversaries."

The vision is the book's final theological statement before the battle: the departed righteous (Onias and Jeremiah) are presented as actively interceding for the living. Onias *prays with outstretched hands for the whole body of the Jews* — the high priest who was murdered (4:34) continues his priestly intercession beyond death. Jeremiah the prophet of the exile gives Judas the sword of divine victory — the prophet who wept over Jerusalem's destruction equips its defenders.

Three theological elements:

**1. The departed intercede**: Onias and Jeremiah are not merely remembered — they are active. The prayer posture (outstretched hands) in the vision is the living prayer-posture of the priestly office: the dead high priest continues the living liturgy.

**2. The sword as divine gift**: *doron apo tou theou* = a gift from God. The military victory is framed as a theological gift — the same as the Holy-War theology of 1 Maccabees 3:18–19. The sword comes from God through the prophetic tradition (Jeremiah) to the warrior (Judas).

**3. Jeremiah as the prophet of Jerusalem's fall / and defender**: The same prophet who prophesied Jerusalem's destruction now arms its defender. The prophetic word that announced judgment is fulfilled; the same prophetic authority now equips the restoration.

**Hebrews 12:1** — "Therefore since we are surrounded by so great a cloud of witnesses (*nephos martyrōn*) let us also lay aside every weight." The Hebrews *nephos martyrōn* = cloud of witnesses is the theological generalization of the 2 Maccabees 15 vision: the departed faithful are witnesses who surround the living struggle. The Maccabean vision of Onias and Jeremiah watching and praying and equipping is the OT narrative of what Hebrews declares as a general theological reality.

**Revelation 6:9–11** — "I saw under the altar the souls of those who had been slain for the word of God and for the witness they had borne — they cried out with a loud voice: O Sovereign Lord holy and true — how long before you will judge and avenge our blood?" The souls under the altar crying out is the eschatological form of the Maccabean vision: the departed martyrs are not silent but actively engaged with the divine purpose. The 2 Maccabees 15 vision of the praying Onias and the equipping Jeremiah is the deuterocanonical type of the Revelation altar-souls.

## STRUCTURAL NOTE — 2 MACCABEES AS THE RESURRECTION BOOK

2 Maccabees is the LXX's theological capstone on the question that has been accumulating since the exile: what happens to those who die faithful to the covenant when the covenant-community is under existential threat? The answer that 1 Maccabees leaves implicit (the martyrs died / they are honored / their example endures) 2 Maccabees makes explicit and theologically grounded:

**The resurrection answers the martyrdom**: The seven brothers do not die as tragic victims — they die as resurrection-confessors. Their deaths are not the end of the covenant-story but the ante-penultimate act before the resurrection. The *anastasōs aiōniou* = everlasting life-renewal is not wishful thinking but a deduction from the theology of creation: the God who made from nothing can restore from death.

**The *creatio ex nihilo* grounds the resurrection**: The mother's speech (7:28) links creation theology to resurrection theology in a single argument. This link — implicit in the Psalms and prophets — is made explicit here for the first time. Paul's deployment of the same link (Romans 4:17) shows the direct line of theological inheritance.

**The discipline-not-destruction theology** (6:12–16) places the entire Maccabean suffering within the covenant-framework: the Lord's *eleos* never fails / the suffering is *paideia* / the end is reconciliation. The book that narrates the most extreme covenant-suffering in the LXX is also the book that states most clearly that the Lord never abandons his *eleos*.

**The place-for-the-nation theology** (5:19) resolves the Temple question: the Temple is not an end in itself but a covenant-sign for the covenant-people. The ultimate Temple — the body of the Son of God — fulfills and supersedes the Maccabean rededication.

**The NT trajectory from 2 Maccabees:**
- *Anastasōs aiōniou* → John 5:28–29 / 11:24–25 / 1 Corinthians 15
- *Ouk ex ontōn epoiēsen* → Romans 4:17 / Hebrews 11:3
- *Paideia* / discipline-not-destruction → Hebrews 12:5–11
- *Eleos* never withdrawn → Romans 8:28 / Romans 11:29
- *Kyrieuonta tēs zōēs kai tou pneumatos* → Acts 17:25 / John 5:21
- Cloud of witnesses praying and equipping → Hebrews 12:1 / Revelation 6:9–11
- Prayer for the dead coherent if resurrection is real → 1 Corinthians 15:29
