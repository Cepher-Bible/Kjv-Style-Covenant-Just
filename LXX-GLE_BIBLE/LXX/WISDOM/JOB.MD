\v 1 There was a man in the land of Uz — his name was Job — and that man was true and blameless — righteous and God-fearing — and keeping himself from every evil thing.
\v 2 And there were born to him seven sons and three daughters.
\v 3 And his livestock were seven thousand sheep and three thousand camels — and five hundred yoke of oxen and five hundred she-donkeys in pasture — and a very large household — and that man was great among those of the east.
\v 4 And his sons went and made a feast by turns each in his house — calling also their three sisters to eat and drink with them.
\v 5 And when the days of the feast had run their course Job sent and purified them — rising early and offering burnt offerings according to their number — for Job said: Perhaps my sons thought evil in their hearts toward God. So Job did continually.
\p
\v 6 And it came to pass on a day that the angels of God came to stand before the Lord — and the devil came among them.
\v 7 And the Lord said to the devil: Where are you coming from? And the devil answered the Lord and said: Going around the earth and walking about in it.
\v 8 And the Lord said to the devil: Have you considered my servant Job — that there is none like him on the earth — a man true and blameless — God-fearing — keeping himself from every evil thing?
\v 9 And the devil answered and said before the Lord: Does Job fear God for nothing?
\v 10 Have you not made a fence around him and around his household and around all that he has on every side? — you blessed the works of his hands and his livestock increased in the land.
\v 11 But stretch out your hand and touch all that he has — and see if he will not bless you to your face.
\v 12 Then the Lord said to the devil: Look — all that he has I give into your hand — but do not lay your hand upon himself. And the devil went out from before the Lord.
\p
\v 13 And it came to pass on a day that Job's sons and daughters were eating and drinking wine in the house of their eldest brother —
\v 14 and a messenger came to Job and said: The oxen were plowing and the she-donkeys were grazing beside them —
\v 15 and the Sabeans came and took them and put the servants to the sword — and I alone escaped to tell you.
\v 16 While he was still speaking another came and said: Fire fell from heaven and burned up the sheep and the servants and consumed them — and I alone escaped to tell you.
\v 17 While he was still speaking another came and said: Chaldeans made three companies and surrounded the camels and took them and put the servants to the sword — and I alone escaped to tell you.
\v 18 While he was still speaking another came and said: Your sons and daughters were eating and drinking wine in the house of their eldest brother —
\v 19 and suddenly a great wind came from the desolate-place and touched the four corners of the house and it fell upon the young men and they died — and I alone escaped to tell you.
\p
\v 20 Then Job arose and tore his robe and shaved his head and fell to the ground and worshipped —
\v 21 and said:
\q1 Naked I came out of my mother's womb —
\q2 and naked I shall return there —
\q1 the Lord gave and the Lord took away —
\q2 as it pleased the Lord so it came to pass —
\q1 blessed be the name of the Lord.
\p
\v 22 In all these things Job did not sin before the Lord — nor charge God with folly.

\v 1 And it came to pass on a day that the angels of God came to stand before the Lord — and the devil came among them to stand before the Lord.
\v 2 And the Lord said to the devil: Where are you coming from? And the devil answered before the Lord and said: Going around the earth and walking about in it.

\p
\v 7 And the devil went out from the Lord and struck Job with a grievous sore from the sole of his foot to the crown of his head.
\v 8 And he took a piece of pottery to scrape himself with — and he sat among the ashes.
\v 9 And much time passed — and his wife said to him: How long will you hold on saying: Look — I will wait a little longer — expecting the hope of my salvation? For look — your memorial has perished from the earth — sons and daughters — the pangs and pains of my womb — for whom I labored in vain with hardships — and you yourself sit in the rottenness of worms — passing the night in the open air. And I am a wanderer and a servant — going about from place to place and from house to house — waiting for when the sun will set so I may rest from the labors and the griefs which now oppress me. But say some word to the Lord and die.
\v 10 But he looked at her and said: You have spoken like one of the foolish women. If we have received good things from the hand of the Lord — shall we not endure evil things? In all these things that happened to him Job did not sin with his lips before God.
\p
\v 11 And Job's three friends heard of all the evil that had come upon him — and they came each one from his own country — Eliphaz the Temanite — and Bildad the Shuhite — and Zophar the Naamathite — and they came together to visit him and comfort him.
\v 12 And when they saw him from a distance they did not recognize him — and they cried out with a loud voice and wept — and each tore his robe and sprinkled dust upon their heads.
\v 13 And they sat with him on the ground seven days and seven nights — and no one spoke a word to him — for they saw that the pain was very great.

## CRITICAL TEXTUAL NOTE — THE LXX JOB

The LXX Job is approximately one-sixth shorter than the MT — the most dramatic divergence between LXX and MT in the entire OT. The LXX translator condensed speeches, omitted strophes, and in places produced a substantially different text. Origen supplemented the LXX from Theodotion to restore the missing material, marking the additions with asterisks. The GLE engine rendered the Rahlfs-Hanhart LXX text — the shorter form — which represents the Greek canonical text as received by the NT authors and the early church.

**Key LXX divergences tracked in notes:**
- Job's wife's speech (2:9) is greatly expanded in LXX — a poignant lament over her own sufferings
- The phoenix reference (29:18) — LXX *phoinix* = phoenix; MT *ḥōl* = sand — a significant divergence in Job's expression of hope
- Chapter 28 wisdom hymn — largely congruent in both traditions
- The divine speeches (38–41) are condensed in LXX but the theological thrust is maintained
- The LXX epilogue (42:17) adds a brief note identifying Job with Jobab of Genesis 36:33 and locating him in the Gentile world

## VOCABULARY DECISIONS — JOB SYSTEMATIC

### *Diabolos* — devil

The LXX renders the Hebrew *śāṭān* = accuser/adversary as *diabolos* = devil throughout the prologue (1:6–2:7). The *śāṭān* = the accuser in the heavenly court — the prosecuting attorney figure — becomes *diabolos* = the slanderer/accuser in Greek. This is the same *diabolos* as in **Wisdom 2:24** — "through the envy of the devil death entered the world" — and as in **1 Peter 5:8** — "your adversary the devil (*diabolos*) walks about like a roaring lion seeking someone to devour." The engine rendered "devil" throughout — not "adversary" or "satan" — preserving the LXX *diabolos* terminology.

### *Pantokratōr* — Almighty

The LXX frequently renders the Hebrew *Shaddai* = the Almighty as *Pantokratōr* = All-Powerful/All-Ruler — the same word used in **Revelation 1:8** — "says the Lord God — who is and who was and who is to come — the Almighty (*Pantokratōr*)." Job is the OT book richest in *Pantokratōr* / *Shaddai* occurrences. The engine rendered "Almighty" throughout.

### *Hades* and *Abaddon*

*Hadēs* = the realm of the dead appears throughout Job (7:9, 10:21–22, 11:8, 14:13, 17:13, 17:16, 21:13, 24:19, 26:6). *Abaddon* = Destruction / the deep abyss (26:6, 28:22, 31:12) — rendered as a proper noun "Abaddon" rather than "Destruction" to preserve the place-name register. Both rendered consistently throughout.

### *Lytrōtēs* / *Redeemer*

**19:25** — *oida gar hoti aenaos estin ho eklytrōsamenos me* = I know that my Redeemer is eternal / my Redeemer lives. The *eklytrōtēs* = Redeemer — from *lytrōō* = to redeem, to ransom. This is the single most important verse in Job for the NT redemption vocabulary. The engine rendered "my Redeemer lives" — the *lytrōtēs* = Redeemer vocabulary preserved.

### *Pneuma* — breath-of-life

The *pneuma* of God as the animating power (32:8 — "the breath of the Almighty gives understanding") and the *pneuma* as Job's inner life and speech throughout. Rendered "breath-of-life" consistently — "the breath-of-life of God has made me" (33:4).

### *Phoinix* — phoenix

**29:18** — "I shall multiply my days as the phoenix (*phoinix*)." The LXX reads the obscure Hebrew *ḥōl* = sand (or possibly a rare word for phoenix) as *phoinix* = phoenix — the mythological bird of resurrection. This is the only phoenix reference in the LXX and is theologically significant: Job expresses hope for life renewed in terms of the phoenix's resurrection. The engine rendered "phoenix" following the LXX.

## CAE NODES — JOB

### Job 1:1 — *alēthinos kai amemptos* / true and blameless — CAE NODE

**1:1** — "There was a man in the land of Uz — his name was Job — and that man was true and blameless — righteous and God-fearing — and keeping himself from every evil thing."

The four-fold characterization of Job — *alēthinos* = true/genuine, *amemptos* = blameless, *dikaios* = righteous, *theosebēs* = God-fearing — establishes the book's central problem: if wisdom-theology is correct (the righteous prosper, the impious suffer) — Job disproves it. The *amemptos* = blameless vocabulary — **Philippians 2:15** — "that you may be blameless (*amemptoi*) and innocent — children of God without blemish."

---

### Job 1:21 — *kyrios edōken ho kyrios apheilato* / the Lord gave and the Lord took away — CAE PRIMARY NODE

**1:21** — "Naked I came out of my mother's womb — and naked I shall return there — the Lord gave and the Lord took away — as it pleased the Lord so it came to pass — blessed be the name of the Lord."

***kyrios edōken ho kyrios apheilato — hōs tō kyriō edoxen houtōs egeneto — eiē to onoma kyriou eulogēmenon*** = the Lord gave and the Lord took away — as it pleased the Lord so it came to pass — blessed be the name of the Lord.

**1 Timothy 6:7** — "for we brought nothing into the world and we cannot take anything out of the world." The Job 1:21 *naked/naked* formula. **James 4:14** — "you do not know what tomorrow will bring — what is your life? For you are a mist that appears for a little time and then vanishes." The *naked-entry/naked-departure* theology of Job 1:21 is the ground for the NT's anti-acquisitive ethic.

The *eulogēmenon to onoma kyriou* = blessed be the name of the Lord — the Joban *berakah* = blessing at the point of maximum suffering — the paradigm of worship through loss.

---

### Job 2:9 — Job's wife — LXX EXPANSION NODE

**2:9** — The LXX greatly expands Job's wife's speech beyond the MT's two lines. The LXX wife speaks a full lament: recounting how long she has waited, describing the loss of their sons and daughters, her own wandering from house to house as a servant, her exhaustion — and then: "But say some word to the Lord and die."

The expanded LXX wife-speech transforms the character from the MT's dismissive figure into a suffering witness — her own loss and hardship articulated before her challenge to Job. The engine rendered the full LXX expansion.

---

### Job 3:3–26 — The Lament — CAE NODE

**3:3** — "Let the day perish on which I was born."

Job's opening curse of his birth-day is the longest sustained lament in the OT outside the Psalter — and is directly paralleled by **Jeremiah 20:14–18** — "Cursed be the day on which I was born." The *ara* = curse of the day of birth — the most extreme form of lament — is the tradition Job and Jeremiah share.

**Revelation 12:4** — the language of birth and cosmic darkness in Job 3 echoes in the cosmic birth-struggle of Revelation 12. The *Leviathan* reference of 3:8 connects to the Job 41 / Revelation 12–13 dragon-sea-monster tradition.

---

### Job 5:13 — *ho katalambanōn sophous en tē phronēsei autōn* / he catches the wise in their own craftiness — CAE NODE

**5:13** — "He catches the wise in their own craftiness — and the counsel of the devious is brought to a quick end."

**1 Corinthians 3:19** — "For the wisdom of this world is foolishness with God — for it is written: He catches the wise in their craftiness (*ho drassomenos tous sophous en tē panourgia autōn*)." Paul cites Job 5:13 directly — one of the rare explicit citations of Job in the NT. The LXX *katalambanō en tē phronēsei* = catches in the cleverness — Paul's *drassomai en tē panourgia* = seizes in the craftiness. The engine rendered "catches the wise in their own craftiness" — the exact vocabulary Paul requires.

---

### Job 7:17–18 — *ti estin anthrōpos* / what is man — CAE NODE

**7:17–18** — "What is man that you make so much of him — and that you set your heart on him — and visit him every morning — and test him every moment?"

***ti estin anthrōpos hoti megalyneis auton*** = what is man that you magnify him. This is an anti-Psalm — a dark inversion of **Psalm 8:4–5** — "What is man that you remember him — and the son of man that you visit him? You made him a little lower than the angels." In Psalm 8 the divine attention is honor; in Job 7 it is torment. **Hebrews 2:6** cites Psalm 8:4–6 — and the Job 7 counter-tradition is the shadow behind it: the same *ti estin anthrōpos* = what is man formula, the same *episkeptomai* = visit vocabulary — but reversed in register.

---

### Job 9:33 — *mesis* / arbiter/mediator — CAE PRIMARY NODE

**9:33** — "There is no arbiter (*mesis*) between us — who might lay his hand on us both."

***ouk estin ho meses hēmōn*** = there is no mediator between us — no one who can lay a hand on both. Job's cry for a mediator — someone who can bridge the infinite distance between the creature and God — is one of the most theologically explosive statements in the OT.

**1 Timothy 2:5** — "For there is one God and one mediator (*mesitēs*) between God and men — the man Christ Jesus." The *mesitēs* = mediator of 1 Timothy 2:5 is the answer to Job 9:33's anguished *ouk estin ho mesis* = there is no mediator. Christ is the one Job cried out for — who can lay his hand on both God and man.

**Hebrews 8:6, 9:15, 12:24** — "Jesus is the mediator (*mesitēs*) of a better covenant" / "mediator of a new covenant" / "Jesus the mediator of the new covenant." The entire Hebrews *mesitēs* theology answers Job 9:33.

The engine rendered "there is no arbiter between us" — *mesis* = arbiter/mediator — the foundational cry preserved.

### Job 13:15 — *ean me apokteina me ep' autō elpioō* / though he slay me I will hope — CAE NODE

**13:15** — "Though he slay me — I will hope in him — yet I will argue my ways to his face."

The LXX rendering of the famously ambiguous Hebrew *lo yḥl* — the MT's *kethib* reads "I will not hope" (*lo* = not) and *qere* reads "I will hope in him" (*lô* = to him). The LXX and the dominant Christian tradition reads "I will hope in him" — the paradox of hope through death. **Romans 8:24** — "For in hope we were saved." **Hebrews 11:1** — "faith is the substance of things hoped for." The Joban hope-through-death that fires in the NT's theology of resurrection-faith.

---

### Job 14:14 — *ean apothanē anthrōpos zēsetai* / if a man dies will he live again — CAE PRIMARY NODE

**14:14** — "If a man dies — will he live again? — all the days of my service I would wait — until my relief should come."

***ean apothanē anthrōpos zēsetai*** = if a man dies will he live? — the great question of the book, spoken at the exact center of the dialogues. The *zēsetai* = will he live? — the resurrection question before resurrection is revealed.

**John 11:25** — "I am the resurrection and the life — whoever believes in me though he die yet shall he live (*zēsetai*)." Jesus's answer to the Lazarus situation uses the *zēsetai* vocabulary of Job 14:14 — the question Job asks is answered by the one who is the resurrection. The engine rendered "if a man dies — will he live again" — the *zēsetai* question preserved.

---

### Job 19:25–27 — *oida hoti aenaos estin ho eklytrōsamenos me* / I know that my Redeemer lives — CAE PRIMARY NODE

**19:25–27** — "For I know that my Redeemer lives — and at the last he will stand upon the earth. And after my skin has been thus destroyed — yet in my flesh I shall see God — whom I shall see for myself — and my eyes shall behold and not another. My kidneys are consumed within me."

***oida gar hoti aenaos estin ho eklytrōsamenos me*** = for I know that my Redeemer is eternal / my Redeemer lives. The LXX renders *ḥay* = living as *aenaos* = eternal/everlasting — a heightening of the Hebrew. The *eklytrōtēs* = Redeemer — from *eklytrōō* = to redeem fully.

**19:26** — the LXX rendering of the famous verse is difficult: *anastēsai to derma mou to antloun tauta* — but the theological thrust is preserved: the flesh/body seeing God after destruction — a bodily resurrection vision.

This is the single most important resurrection text in all the Wisdom literature — Job's climactic confession of *bodily* vindication beyond death:

**1 Corinthians 15:52–55** — "the dead will be raised incorruptible... this mortal must put on immortality." The Joban *eklytrōtēs* = Redeemer who stands at the last = *eschatos* on the earth is the ground for the NT's eschatological Redeemer-Christology.

**Revelation 1:17–18** — "I am the first and the last — and the living one (*ho zōn*) — I was dead — and look — I am alive to the ages of the ages." The *ho zōn* = the living one of Revelation echoes the *eklytrōsamenos* who is *aenaos* = eternal/living of Job 19:25. The engine rendered "I know that my Redeemer lives" — the *aenaos* = eternal/lives preserved.

### Job 28:28 — *he tou theou sophia* / the fear of the Lord that is wisdom — CAE NODE

**28:28** — "And he said to man: Look — the fear of the Lord — that is wisdom — and to turn away from evil is understanding."

***idou hē tou theou sophia* — the wisdom of God** — the LXX reads *theosebeia* = godliness/fear of God as wisdom. The wisdom hymn (chapter 28) concludes: wisdom is inaccessible to human searching — God alone knows its place — and what God has declared to man is: fear him and flee evil.

The full circle of the wisdom tradition: Proverbs 1:7 (*archē sophias phobos kyriou*) → Sirach 1:14 → Job 28:28 — all three declaring the *phobos kyriou* = fear of the Lord as the *archē* / *telos* of wisdom. **James 1:5** — ask God for wisdom. **1 Corinthians 1:24** — Christ is the wisdom of God (*Christos theou sophia*). The Job 28 wisdom hymn is the OT's deepest meditation on divine wisdom's inaccessibility — answered in the NT by the incarnation.

### Job 33:23–24 — *angelos mesitēs* / an angel mediator — CAE NODE

**33:23–24** — "If there be for him an angel — a mediator — one of the thousand — to declare to man what is right for him — and he is merciful to him and says: Deliver him from going down into the pit — I have found a ransom."

***ean ōsin chilioi angeloi thanatēphoroi — heis autōn ou mē trōsē auton — ean noēsē en tē kardia epistrepsai pros kyrion*** → Elihu's vision of the angel-mediator who finds a *lytron* = ransom to deliver from the pit.

**1 Timothy 2:5–6** — "one mediator between God and men — the man Christ Jesus — who gave himself as a ransom (*antilytron*) for all." The *lytron* = ransom of Job 33:24 → the *antilytron* = ransom of 1 Timothy 2:6. Elihu's angel-mediator who finds a ransom anticipates the NT's Mediator who *is* the ransom.

**Mark 10:45** — "For even the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve — and to give his life as a ransom (*lytron*) for many." The *lytron* vocabulary of Job 33:24 fires directly in the Markan ransom-saying.

---

### Job 38:1–40:2 — The Whirlwind Speeches — CAE PRIMARY NODE

**38:1–7** — "Then the Lord answered Job out of the whirlwind and said: Who is this that darkens counsel by words without knowledge?... Where were you when I laid the foundations of the earth?... when the morning stars sang together — and all the sons of God shouted for joy?"

The divine speeches are the theological climax of the entire book — and one of the supreme literary achievements of the OT. The creation-catechism of chapters 38–39 — the cascading *where were you* / *can you* / *do you know* questions — establishes the infinite distance between Creator and creature.

**Romans 11:34** — "For who has known the mind of the Lord — or who has been his counselor?" Paul's doxology in Romans 11:33–36 draws on the whirlwind speech vocabulary: the unsearchable (*anexeraunēta*) and inscrutable (*anexichniastoi*) ways of God — **Job 9:10** — "he does great things beyond searching out (*anexichniastous*) — and marvelous things beyond number." **Romans 11:33** uses the exact *anexichniastos* vocabulary of Job.

**Revelation 4–5** — the throne-room vision draws on the heavenly-court imagery of Job 1–2 and the creation-praise of Job 38:7 — "the morning stars sang together and all the sons of God shouted for joy" → the four living creatures and the elders crying "Holy — holy — holy."

---

### Job 40:15–41:34 — Behemoth and Leviathan — CAE NODE

**40:15** — "Look now at Behemoth — which I made as I made you — he eats grass like an ox."

**41:1** — "Can you draw out Leviathan with a fishhook — or press down his tongue with a cord?"

**41:34** — "He sees everything that is high — he is king over all the sons of pride."

The Behemoth and Leviathan speeches — the two great chaos-monsters at the limits of creation — are the answer to Job's implicit complaint that God has treated him like a chaos-monster requiring guard (7:12 — "Am I a sea or a sea monster — that you set a guard over me?"). God's answer: the chaos-monsters are his creatures — and he alone controls them.

**Revelation 12–13** — the dragon and the beast from the sea draw on the Leviathan imagery of Job 41 and **Isaiah 27:1** — "the Lord with his great and strong sword will punish Leviathan the fleeing serpent — Leviathan the twisting serpent — and he will slay the dragon that is in the sea." The Leviathan = chaos-serpent-sea-monster = the ancient adversary is the deep background for Revelation's dragon-beast cosmology.

**Psalm 74:14** — "You crushed the heads of Leviathan." The divine crushing of chaos is the victory Job 41 describes from the perspective of God's sovereign mastery.

---

### Job 42:7–8 — God's verdict — CAE PRIMARY NODE

**42:7–8** — "My anger burns against you and against your two friends — for you have not spoken of me what is right — as my servant Job has."

The divine verdict overturns the friends' entire theological program. The friends spoke *theologically correctly* — retribution theology, divine justice, human sinfulness — but they spoke *wrongly about God*. Job spoke in anguish, accusation, and protest — but spoke *rightly about God*. The friends' correct theology was false witness; Job's anguished protest was true witness.

**James 5:11** — "You have heard of the endurance of Job — and you have seen the end (*telos*) of the Lord — that the Lord is compassionate and merciful." The *hypomonē Iōb* = the endurance of Job is the NT's summary of the book's lesson — not Job's patience (he was not patient) but his *endurance* — his refusal to abandon his case before God.

---

### Job 42:10–17 — The Restoration — CAE NODE

**42:10** — "And the Lord restored the fortunes of Job — when he had prayed for his friends — and the Lord gave Job twice as much as he had before."

The restoration comes *through intercession* — Job prays for those who wronged him — and the restoration follows. **James 5:15–16** — "the prayer of faith will save the sick man... The prayer of a righteous person has great power as it is working." The Joban intercession-and-restoration pattern underlies the James 5 teaching on prayer.

**42:17 LXX ADDITION** — The LXX closes with a genealogical note: "And it is written that he will rise again with those whom the Lord raises up." This unique LXX addition is a resurrection affirmation absent from the MT — the LXX translator or a later hand added the explicit *anastēsetai* = he will rise again vocabulary to Job's ending.

## THEOLOGICAL NOTE — JOB IN THE NT

Job is the OT book that most directly confronts the central theodicy question — the suffering of the righteous — and provides no easy answer. The NT receives Job's theology through several channels:

**1. The mediator-cry (9:33) answered by Christ** — "there is no mediator" → "there is one mediator — Christ Jesus" (1 Tim 2:5)

**2. The Redeemer-who-lives (19:25) identified with Christ** — *aenaos eklytrōtēs* → the risen Christ as the eternal Redeemer

**3. The ransom-mediator (33:23–24) fulfilled in Christ** — angel-mediator finding a ransom → the Son of Man giving his life as *lytron*

**4. The wisdom-inaccessibility (28:12–28) resolved in the incarnation** — wisdom beyond human finding → Christ as the wisdom of God (1 Cor 1:24)

**5. The endurance-of-Job (James 5:11)** — the model of persistent honest prayer through suffering

**6. The whirlwind speeches (38–39)** — the ground for Paul's *who has known the mind of the Lord* doxology (Rom 11:33–36)

**7. The Leviathan (41) as the chaos-adversary** — the deep background for Revelation's dragon-beast cosmology

The book's deepest theological contribution: honest protest before God — even accusation — is not impiety but faith. The friends' smooth theology was the lie; Job's anguished wrestling was the truth. The NT inherits this: the cry of dereliction (*My God my God why have you forsaken me* — Psalm 22:1 / Matthew 27:46) is the ultimate Job-speech — God-forsakenness cried out to God — which is itself faith.
