## CRITICAL TEXTUAL NOTE — LXX LAMENTATIONS

The LXX Lamentations (*Thrēnoi*) closely follows the MT with no major structural divergences — unlike Jeremiah or Ezekiel. The primary translation challenge is the acrostic structure: chapters 1–4 are alphabetic acrostics in Hebrew (each verse or stanza beginning with successive letters of the Hebrew alphabet — chapter 3 uses a triple acrostic). The acrostic is invisible in the LXX translation but its presence governs the theology: the full alphabet = totality of suffering — the complete A-to-Z of grief is laid before God.

The book is attributed in the LXX tradition to Jeremiah — the Rahlfs-Hanhart LXX titles it *Thrēnoi Ieremiou* = Lamentations of Jeremiah. The opening verses of the LXX Lamentations include a prose superscription absent from the MT: "And it came to pass after Israel was taken captive and Jerusalem made desolate that Jeremiah sat weeping and lamented with this lamentation over Jerusalem."

## CAE NODES — LAMENTATIONS

### Lamentations 1:12 — *ei estin odynē* / is there any sorrow — CAE PRIMARY NODE

**1:12** — "Is it nothing to you — all you who pass by? — look and see — if there is any sorrow like my sorrow — which was brought upon me — which the Lord inflicted — on the day of his fierce anger."

***ei estin odynē kata tēn odynēn mou*** = if there is sorrow like my sorrow. The cry of abandoned Zion addressed to passers-by who ignore her suffering — asking whether any grief equals hers.

The patristic and liturgical tradition from the earliest centuries applied Lamentations 1:12 to the Passion of Christ — the suffering servant asking the same question from the cross: *is there any sorrow like my sorrow?* The verse became one of the primary Passion-meditation texts in Christian liturgy (Tenebrae / Holy Week offices).

**Isaiah 53:3–4** — "a man of sorrows and acquainted with grief... surely he has borne our griefs and carried our sorrows." The *odynē* = sorrow / pain vocabulary of Lamentations 1:12 and the Isaiah 53 *odynai* = sorrows form a single Passion-lament tradition. Both texts describe the suffering of the righteous/representative figure whose pain is unmatched and largely unacknowledged.

**Matthew 27:39** — "those who passed by (*hoi de paraporeuomenoi*) derided him." The *paraporeuomenoi* = those passing by of Matthew 27:39 are the exact *hoi paraporeuomenoi* = those passing by of Lamentations 1:12 LXX. The verbal link is precise — the passers-by who see the affliction and do not respond with compassion.

### Lamentations 3:1–18 — *anēr ho horōn* / the man who has seen affliction — CAE NODE

**3:1** — "I am the man who has seen affliction under the rod of his wrath."

***egō eimi ho anēr ho horōn ptōcheian*** = I am the man who has seen poverty/affliction. The individual *anēr* = man of chapter 3 shifts from the corporate feminine voice of Zion (chapters 1–2) to a singular male sufferer — the *geber* = strong man reduced to nothing. The most intense personal lament in the OT.

**3:6** — "He has made me dwell in darkness like the dead of long ago." **Psalm 87:7 (LXX)** — "you have put me in the lowest pit — in darkness — in the depths." The darkness/pit-of-the-dead vocabulary running through 3:1–18 is the most sustained OT pre-figuration of the *descent into Hades* tradition. **1 Peter 3:19** — "he went and proclaimed to the spirits in prison."

**3:14** — "I have become the laughingstock of all peoples — the object of their taunts all day long." **Psalm 21:7 (LXX)** — "all who see me mock me — they make mouths at me — they wag their heads." The mocking-sufferer register shared between Lamentations 3:14 / Psalm 22 / Matthew 27:39–44.

### Lamentations 3:22–24 — *eleos Kyriou* / steadfast love never ceases — CAE PRIMARY NODE

**3:22–24** — "The steadfast love of the Lord never ceases — his mercies never come to an end — they are new every morning — great is your faithfulness. The Lord is my portion — says my soul — therefore I will hope in him."

***ta elē Kyriou hoti ouk exelipen*** = the mercies of the Lord — for they have not ceased. The theological center of the entire book — emerging from the deepest darkness of chapter 3 at verse 22. The book's acrostic structure places these verses at the exact mathematical center (chapter 3 = center chapter / verse 22 = center pivot).

***eleos*** = steadfast love / mercy / covenant loyalty — the *hesed* of the Hebrew rendered *eleos* in the LXX. This is the fundamental divine attribute of the entire OT covenant.

**Hebrews 13:5** — "I will never leave you nor forsake you — so we can confidently say: The Lord is my helper." **Romans 8:38–39** — "I am sure that neither death nor life... nor anything else in all creation will be able to separate us from the love (*agapē*) of God in Christ Jesus our Lord." Paul's *nothing can separate us* is the NT expansion of Lamentations 3:22 *eleos never ceases*.

**James 1:17** — "every good gift and every perfect gift is from above... with whom there is no variation or shadow due to change." The *great is your faithfulness* of Lamentations 3:23 underlies the Jamesian immutability-of-the-good-God theology.

The *new every morning* (*kaina to prōi*) = **Revelation 21:5** — "look — I am making all things new (*kaina poiō panta*)." The daily-renewal logic of Lamentations 3:23 is the morning-face of the eschatological new-creation promise.

### Lamentations 3:25–30 — *chrēstos Kyrios* / the Lord is good to those who wait — CAE NODE

**3:25–30** — "The Lord is good to those who wait for him — to the soul that seeks him. It is good that one should wait quietly for the salvation of the Lord... Let him give his cheek to the one who strikes and let him be filled with insults."

***chrēstos Kyrios tois hypomenousin auton*** = the Lord is good to those who endure/wait for him.

**3:30** — "Let him give his cheek to the one who strikes." 

**Matthew 5:39** — "if anyone slaps you on the right cheek — turn to him the other also." The Sermon on the Mount's cheek-turning instruction draws on the Lamentations 3:30 *cheek-to-the-striker* posture of patient suffering under divine discipline. The text presents it not as passive resignation but as the posture of the one who trusts the Lord's ultimate vindication (3:31–33 — "the Lord will not cast off forever").

**Isaiah 50:6** — "I gave my back to those who strike and my cheeks to those who pull out the beard — I hid not my face from disgrace and spitting." The Servant Song cheek-giving and the Lamentations 3:30 cheek-giving form a single *endurance-under-suffering* tradition that Jesus explicitly teaches.

---

### Lamentations 3:55–58 — *ek bothrou* / from the depths of the pit — CAE NODE

**3:55–58** — "I called on your name O Lord from the depths of the pit. You heard my plea: Do not close your ear to my cry for help. You came near when I called on you — you said: Do not fear. You have taken up my cause O Lord — you have redeemed my life."

***ek bothrou katotatou eboēsa to onoma sou Kyrie*** = from the lowest pit I called your name O Lord. The prayer from the pit — heard and answered — the suffering *anēr* rescued by divine near-coming.

**Psalm 129:1 (LXX)** — "out of the depths (*ek batheon*) I cry to you O Lord." The *de profundis* prayer tradition. **Jonah 2:3** — "out of the belly of Hades (*ek koilias hadou*) I cried." All three — Lamentations 3:55 / Psalm 130 / Jonah 2 — are *from-the-pit* prayers answered by divine rescue that become NT resurrection types.

**3:57** — "You came near (*ēngisas*) when I called on you." **James 4:8** — "draw near to God and he will draw near to you (*eggiei hymin*)." The divine *engizō* = drawing near in response to the cry from the depths — the Lamentations 3:57 rescue becomes the James 4:8 promise.

---

### Lamentations 4:20 — *christos Kyriou* / the Lord's anointed — CAE NODE

**4:20** — "The breath of our nostrils — the Lord's anointed — was captured in their pits — of whom we said: Under his shadow we shall live among the nations."

***christos Kyriou*** = the Lord's anointed / Christ of the Lord. The *christos Kyriou* = anointed of the Lord captured and imprisoned — the covenant king taken in the enemy's trap.

In the immediate historical context: the capture of Zedekiah. But the LXX *christos Kyriou* vocabulary makes the Christological resonance unavoidable — the anointed one captured, the one under whose shadow the nations would shelter (Ezekiel 17:23 / Daniel 4:12 mustard-tree) now himself a prisoner.

**Luke 2:26** — *Christon Kyriou* = the Lord's anointed — Simeon told he would not die before seeing "the Lord's anointed." The exact *Christos Kyriou* phrase of Lamentations 4:20 — the suffering anointed replaced by the glorified anointed.

---

### Lamentations 5:19–22 — *sy de Kyrie eis ton aiōna* / but you O Lord reign forever — CAE PRIMARY NODE

**5:19–22** — "But you O Lord reign forever — your throne endures to all generations. Why do you forget us forever — why do you forsake us for so long? Restore us to yourself O Lord — that we may be restored — renew our days as of old — unless you have utterly rejected us — and you remain exceedingly angry with us."

***sy de Kyrie eis ton aiōna katōkēsas*** = but you O Lord — you have dwelt forever. The book ends not in resolution but in unresolved petition — the final verse is grammatically ambiguous: either a question ("have you utterly rejected us?") or a conditional ("unless you have utterly rejected us"). The LXX preserves the same ambiguity.

This deliberate open ending is theologically profound — Lamentations refuses cheap resolution. The book ends with the cry *restore us* (*epistrephō*) and the honest acknowledgment that God may be *utterly angry*. The *epistrepson hēmas* = restore/turn us is the same verb as *shub* = return/repent — the prayer for restoration is simultaneously the prayer for repentance.

**Revelation 22:17** — "The Breath-of-Life and the bride say: Come." The eschatological cry of the Apocalypse is the final resolution of the Lamentations 5:21 *restore us* — the return-prayer of the exiled community answered in the new creation.

**Acts 3:19–21** — "Repent therefore and turn back (*epistrepsate*) that your sins may be blotted out — that times of refreshing (*anapsyxeōs*) may come from the presence of the Lord." The *epistrepsate* = turn back of Acts 3:19 echoes the *epistrepson hēmas* = restore us of Lamentations 5:21.

## VOCABULARY DECISIONS — LAMENTATIONS

### *Eleos* — steadfast love / mercy
Rendered "steadfast love" at 3:22 — *ta elē tou Kyriou* = the steadfast loves / mercies of the Lord. The *eleos* here carries the full *hesed* weight of covenant loyalty — not merely emotional compassion but faithfulness to the covenant commitment. The plural *elē* = mercies (plural acts of mercy) preserved.

### *Thrēnos* — lamentation / dirge
The book's LXX title is *Thrēnoi* = Dirges/Lamentations. **Matthew 2:18** cites Jeremiah 31:15 "Rachel weeping (*thrēnos*)" using the *thrēnos* vocabulary — the Lamentations tradition of inconsolable grief. **Revelation 18:19** — "they threw dust on their heads and cried out — weeping and mourning (*penthountes kai klaiontes*)" — the Babylon-fall lament echoes the Lamentations structure directly.

### *Anēr horōn* — man seeing affliction (3:1)
Rendered "man who has seen affliction" — the *anēr* = strong man / warrior reduced to *ptōcheia* = poverty/affliction. The individual masculine voice of chapter 3 is distinct from the feminine *Zion* voice of chapters 1–2 and 4. This masculine sufferer is the hinge figure — the *geber* = strong man — whose personal lament contains the book's theological center (3:22–24).

### *Christos Kyriou* — the Lord's anointed (4:20)
Rendered "Lord's anointed" — preserving the technical messianic vocabulary. Never softened to "king" or "ruler."

## STRUCTURAL NOTE — LAMENTATIONS AS LITURGICAL GRIEF

Lamentations is the OT's most formally structured grief-text. Five poems — four acrostic — organized around the fall of Jerusalem in 587 BC. But its canonical function extends far beyond the historical moment:

**The book is a theology of lament itself** — it gives the devastated community permission and form for their grief. The genius of the acrostic is that it structures the unbearable — going from *Aleph* to *Tav* = A to Z of pain — because God can bear the full alphabet of human suffering laid before him.

**Chapter 3** is the theological pivot. The book moves from pure lament (1–2) → personal lament deepening to despair (3:1–20) → the *pivot* at 3:21 ("But this I call to mind — therefore I have hope") → renewed lament incorporating hope (3:22–66) → communal lament (4–5). The pivot is not resolution — it is the decision to *call to mind* the *eleos* of the Lord in the midst of unresolved darkness.

**NT use of Lamentations:** The book is less frequently cited directly but pervasively present in the Passion narrative — the *passers-by* / *mocking* / *sorrow like my sorrow* cluster of 1:12 / 2:15 shapes the Matthew 27 crucifixion scene. The *Thrēnoi* tradition directly informs **Revelation 18** (the great lament over Babylon-fallen — echoing Jerusalem-fallen).

**Jewish liturgical use:** Lamentations is read on Tisha B'Av (the ninth of Av) — the fast commemorating the destruction of both Temples. Its canonical position in the Hebrew Bible (Ketuvim / Writings) differs from the LXX where it follows Jeremiah — reinforcing the Jeremianic attribution.
