The "golden thread" that runs from Genesis to Revelation: Name (Internal Character/Reputation) and Image (External Representation/Reflection) are two sides of the same coin.
In the ancient world, a King would set up an Image (a statue) of himself in a distant land to represent his Name (his authority and reputation) where he wasn't physically present.
Here is a structured breakdown of this "Cord of Identity" written for a repository or study guide.
📂 Repository: The Theology of Name & Image
Theme: The Intertwined Cord of Character and Representation
1. The Definitions
 * Name (Hebrew: Shem): This isn't just a label; it’s a legal and moral "Brand." It encompasses a person's reputation, their authority, and their history. To act "in someone's name" is to act as their authorized agent.
 * Image (Hebrew: Tselem): This literally means a "shadow" or "representative figure." Being made in the "Image of God" (Imago Dei) means humans were designed to be the physical mirrors of God’s invisible character on Earth.
2. The Connection: "The Mirror and the Model"
The "Image" is the vessel, and the "Name" is the content.
 * The Problem of Sin: When the Bible talks about humanity falling, it describes the Image becoming "marred" or cracked. Because the mirror is broken, it can no longer accurately reflect the Name (the goodness/justice/mercy) of God to the world.
 * The Commandment: "Do not take the name of the Lord in vain" isn't just about swearing. In the context of Name/Image, it means: Do not represent God's Name falsely while claiming to be His Image. It's about "carrying" the name poorly.
3. The Synergy: Name & Image in Action

|---|---|---|
| The Name | The Source of Authority | "The Lord, the Lord, the compassionate and gracious God..." (Exodus 34:6) |
| The Image | The Display of Authority | "Let us make mankind in our image... so they may rule..." (Genesis 1:26) |
| The Cord | Vocation | Humans are tasked with "naming" creation, using God's authority to bring order, reflecting His "Name." |
4. The "Perfect Cord": Jesus Christ
In the New Testament, this thread reaches its peak. Jesus is described as both:
 * The Perfect Image: "The Son is the radiance of God’s glory and the exact representation of his being" (Hebrews 1:3).
 * The Highest Name: "God exalted him to the highest place and gave him the name that is above every name" (Philippians 2:9).
> The Result: In Christ, the Image is finally "un-broken," allowing the Name of God to be seen clearly by humanity for the first time since the beginning.

📂 Repository Addendum: Naming as Sovereign Proxy
1. The Delegation of Sovereignty
In the ancient Near East, the right to name something was the right to rule it. By allowing Adam to name the animals, God was effectively saying: "I am the High King, but I am making you the Governor. You speak for Me here."
 * God’s Names: God named the big things (Day, Night, Sky, Land, Sea).
 * Humanity’s Names: God left the specific "details" of the earth for humans to name.
 * The Cord: The Image (Adam) uses the Authority (The Name/Word) to bring Order (The Kingdom).
2. Naming = Defining Essence
In the Hebrew mindset, you couldn't name something until you understood its nature. To name an "Ox" an "Ox," Adam had to observe its strength, its behavior, and its place in the world.
 * The Reflection: Just as God defined the essence of the universe by speaking it into existence, the Image-bearer defines the "flavor" of the world by naming it.
 * The Responsibility: If the Image-bearer is corrupt, they name things falsely (calling "evil" good, or "bitter" sweet). This "drags the Name through the mud."
3. The Co-Creative Partnership
This is where the "Image" and "Name" cord gets really tight. God created the potential for the animals, but He waited for the human response to finalize their identity in the earthly realm.
> "Whatever the man called each living creature, that was its name." (Genesis 2:19)
> 
God legally bound Himself to the human’s decision. This shows the incredible weight of being an Image-bearer; God respects the names we give to the things under our care.
📝 Summary: The Naming Mandate
> "To name is to claim. When we name things according to God's character, we are functioning as a clear mirror (Image). When we misname or 'take the Name in vain,' we distort the reality of the world around us."
> 
Why this matters for "God's Name's Sake"
When God acts "for His name's sake," He is often restoring the right names to things. He steps in to call "justice" justice and "sin" sin, because His Image-bearers have stopped doing it correctly.
This cord of Name and Image actually explains why "The Word" (Jesus) had to come to earth. Since the first "Namers" (Adam and Eve) failed, the "Word" came to rename and restore everything.

In the Bible, the phrase "for His name's sake" (or "for God's name's sake") is deeply tied to how ancient cultures understood names. In biblical times, a name was much more than just a label used to get someone's attention. A person's name represented their character, their reputation, their authority, and their very essence.
Therefore, God’s "name" represents the sum total of who He is—His justice, mercy, power, and faithfulness. When the Bible says God does something "for His name's sake," it generally means He is acting for a few specific reasons:
1. To Uphold His Reputation
God acts in ways that reveal His true character to the world so that people will know who He is. If He makes a promise to protect His people and then lets them be destroyed without cause, surrounding nations might think He is weak or unfaithful. By rescuing or providing for them, He defends His reputation as a powerful and faithful God.
2. To Keep His Promises (Covenant Faithfulness)
God made specific covenants (binding promises) with figures like Abraham, Moses, and David. When the Israelites failed or sinned, they technically deserved to be abandoned. However, God continually forgave them and preserved them "for His name's sake"—meaning He stepped in because He is a promise-keeper, regardless of human failures.
3. Acting Out of His Own Character
When God saves, forgives, or guides, He does it because it is His very nature to be good and merciful. He doesn't bless people simply because they have earned it; He does it because of who He is.
Examples in Scripture
 * Guidance: "He refreshes my soul. He guides me along the right paths for his name’s sake." (Psalm 23:3). Here, God acts as the Good Shepherd to show His protective, guiding character.
 * Forgiveness: "For the sake of your name, Lord, forgive my iniquity, though it is great." (Psalm 25:11). The writer is asking for forgiveness not because they deserve it, but because God is inherently merciful.
 * Protection: "For the sake of his great name the Lord will not reject his people, because the Lord was pleased to make you his own." (1 Samuel 12:22). God protects His people to demonstrate His loyalty to the promises He made to them.
In short, "for God's name's sake" means God is acting to display His glory, maintain His reputation, and prove that He is exactly who He claims to be.

In the original Hebrew text of the Old Testament, God is referred to by several different names and titles. Because a name represented a person's character, each of these names reveals a different facet of who God is, how He interacts with humanity, and what He provides.
Here is a look at the foundational names of God, followed by the compound names that describe His specific actions.
The Foundational Names
 * Yahweh (YHWH): The Self-Existent One / The Covenant God
   * Meaning: Often translated as "I AM WHO I AM." It is the personal, sacred name of God. In most English Bibles, it is written as LORD (in all capital letters) to distinguish it from the standard word for lord.
   * Significance: It signifies that God is eternal, self-sufficient, and the keeper of His covenants. When God revealed this name to Moses at the burning bush (Exodus 3:14), He was declaring that He is the absolute, uncreated reality.
 * Elohim: The Creator and Supreme God
   * Meaning: Translated simply as "God." Interestingly, Elohim is grammatically plural in Hebrew, but it is paired with singular verbs.
   * Significance: This plural of majesty denotes supreme, infinite power. It is the very first name of God used in the Bible: "In the beginning, Elohim created the heavens and the earth" (Genesis 1:1). It highlights His role as the powerful Creator of the universe.
 * Adonai: The Master and Sovereign Ruler
   * Meaning: Translated as "Lord" (capital L, lowercase ord). It means Master, Owner, or Sovereign Ruler.
   * Significance: Because the name Yahweh was considered too sacred to speak aloud by ancient Jewish scholars, they would substitute the word Adonai when reading the Scriptures. It acknowledges God's ultimate authority and ownership over our lives.
The "El" Titles (Traits of God)
The prefix El is the singular form of Elohim and is often attached to another word to describe a specific attribute of God.
 * El Shaddai (God Almighty): Emphasizes God’s ultimate power and His ability to nourish, supply, and satisfy His people. It is the name God used when confirming His covenant with Abraham (Genesis 17:1).
 * El Elyon (God Most High): Highlights God’s supremacy above all other "gods," kings, and earthly powers. He is the supreme sovereign (Genesis 14:19).
 * El Roi (The God Who Sees): A deeply personal name given to God by Hagar, an outcast servant who fled into the desert. It reveals that God is not distant; He intimately sees and cares for the marginalized and hurting (Genesis 16:13).
Compound Names of Yahweh
Often, when God revealed a new aspect of His character to the Israelites, they would combine His personal name (Yahweh/Jehovah) with a word describing what He had just done for them.
| Name | Meaning | Key Biblical Context |
|---|---|---|
| Yahweh Yireh (Jehovah Jireh) | The LORD Will Provide | Abraham named the place where God provided a ram to be sacrificed in place of his son Isaac (Gen. 22:14). |
| Yahweh Rapha (Jehovah Rapha) | The LORD Who Heals | God promised the Israelites that if they obeyed Him, He would be their healer (Ex. 15:26). |
| Yahweh Shalom (Jehovah Shalom) | The LORD is Peace | Gideon built an altar and gave it this name after realizing he was in God's presence and would not die, feeling ultimate peace (Judges 6:24). |
| Yahweh Nissi (Jehovah Nissi) | The LORD is My Banner | Moses built an altar under this name after God gave them victory in battle, showing God is their rallying point (Ex. 17:15). |
| Yahweh Roi (Jehovah Raah) | The LORD is My Shepherd | Used by David in Psalm 23, illustrating God's tender care, guidance, and protection. |
Knowing these names helps clarify why God acts "for His name's sake"—because He is defending His identity as the Ultimate Provider, Healer, and Shepherd.

The transition from the Old Testament names of God to the New Testament is one of the most significant "reveals" in the Bible. It effectively bridges the gap between God’s glory and His closeness to humanity.
Here is how the names of God find their ultimate expression in the New Testament:
1. The Name "Jesus" (Yeshua)
The name Jesus isn’t just a common name from the first century; it is a compound name that literally means "Yahweh is Salvation" or "Yahweh Saves." When the angel told Joseph to name the child Jesus, he added the reason: "because he will save his people from their sins" (Matthew 1:21). By giving Him this name, the New Testament declares that the God of the Old Testament (Yahweh) is now acting personally to rescue humanity.
2. Immanuel: "God With Us"
In the Old Testament, God’s presence was often found in a pillar of fire or behind a thick curtain in the Temple. In the New Testament, He receives the title Immanuel (Isaiah 7:14; Matthew 1:23).
 * El (God) + Immanu (with us).
 * This marks a shift from God being "The Most High" (El Elyon) to God being "Right Here."
3. The Great "I AM"
Remember how God identified Himself to Moses as "I AM" (Yahweh)? In the Gospel of John, Jesus uses this specific phrasing seven times to claim the attributes of God for Himself. He essentially takes those "Compound Names" of Yahweh we discussed and applies them to His own ministry:
 * "I am the bread of life" (Matches Yahweh Yireh – The Lord Provides).
 * "I am the good shepherd" (Matches Yahweh Roi – The Lord is my Shepherd).
 * "I am the light of the world" (Matches Yahweh Nissi – The Lord is my Banner/Light).
Why the New Testament focuses on "The Name"
In the New Testament, the "name’s sake" theme continues, but it centers on the authority of Jesus. The early Christians were told that:
 * Salvation is found in no other name (Acts 4:12).
 * Prayer should be done "in His name" (John 14:13).
 * Every knee will bow at the mention of His name (Philippians 2:9-11).
The Shift to "Abba"
Perhaps the most radical change is that Jesus invites His followers to address the "God Almighty" (El Shaddai) as Abba.
 * Abba is an Aramaic word that is best translated as a term of endearment, similar to "Father" or "Papa."
 * It signifies that because of Jesus, the "Name" that was once too holy to even speak (Yahweh) has become a name that children can use to call out to their Father.

The story of Hagar and the name El Roi is one of the most emotional and counter-cultural moments in the entire Bible. It’s the only time a human—and a marginalized, runaway slave woman at that—gives God a name that remains part of His identity forever.
The Context: A Woman on the Run
Hagar was an Egyptian servant to Sarah (Abraham's wife). Because Sarah couldn't have children, she pressured Abraham into a surrogate arrangement with Hagar. When Hagar became pregnant, the relationship turned toxic. Sarah treated her so harshly that Hagar eventually fled into the harsh, blistering desert.
At this point, Hagar is at her lowest:
 * She is a foreigner (Egyptian).
 * She is a slave with no rights.
 * She is homeless and pregnant in a wasteland.
The Encounter: "Where are you going?"
In Genesis 16, the "Angel of the Lord" finds her by a spring in the desert. Interestingly, God doesn't address her as a nameless servant; He calls her by name: "Hagar, slave of Sarai, where have you come from, and where are you going?"
After promising her that her son (Ishmael) would become a great nation, Hagar realizes she isn't just a "cog in the machine" of Abraham’s family. She realizes the Creator of the Universe is looking directly at her.
The Name: El Roi (The God Who Sees)
In response to this encounter, Hagar gives God a name:
> "She gave this name to the Lord who spoke to her: 'You are El Roi,' for she said, 'I have now seen the One who sees me.'" (Genesis 16:13)
> 
Why This Name Matters "For His Name's Sake"
 * Visibility to the Invisible: In ancient times, gods were often thought to care only about kings or priests. Hagar discovers that God's "name" (His character) includes seeing the person everyone else overlooks.
 * Personal Connection: She doesn't just call Him "The All-Seeing One." She calls Him "The God who sees me."
 * God's Reputation: By including this story, the Bible establishes that God's reputation (His name's sake) is built on His attention to the suffering and the outcast.
The Name of her Son: Ishmael
To drive the point home, God tells her to name her son Ishmael, which means "God Hears." Between El Roi (The God Who Sees) and Ishmael (The God Who Hears), Hagar’s story tells us that God’s very identity is tied to His senses being tuned toward human pain.
Comparison: Gideon and Yahweh Shalom
The other "naming" story is quite different. Gideon was a man hiding in a winepress because he was terrified of an invading army. When God called him a "mighty warrior," Gideon was confused and afraid. After God reassured him, Gideon built an altar and called it Yahweh Shalom—"The Lord is Peace."
While Hagar’s story is about being seen in her pain, Gideon’s story is about finding peace in his fear.
